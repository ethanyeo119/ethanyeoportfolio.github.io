About me

I'm Ethan, an Earth Science and GIS student at the University of Auckland, studying from 2025 to 2027.

I'm interested in how landscapes, ecosystems and communities interact, and in using spatial data to look at those questions clearly. My projects so far range from interpolating air pollution and microclimate data to suitability modelling, access analysis and reading physical landscapes.

Most of my coursework has been in ArcGIS Pro and QGIS, working with vector and raster data. I enjoy the practical side too: cleaning and preparing data, building workflows that can be repeated, and finishing with maps that are easy to read.

Outside class, I've supported wetland restoration and waste reduction as an intern with Waiheke Resources Trust, and helped organise events and environmental initiatives on campus with Te Rōpū Taiao, the Environment Association.

Auckland atmospheric pollution analysis

How can monitoring-station measurements be used to map the spatial variation of NO2 and SO2 across Auckland, and where do both pollutants exceed selected thresholds?

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • IDW interpolation
  • Reclassification
  • Raster overlay
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro
Outputs
Two concentration surfaces and a combined-exceedance map
Read the case study

Overview

I used readings from air quality monitoring stations to estimate how nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and sulphur dioxide (SO2) vary across Auckland, then identified locations where both pollutants exceed thresholds I selected for the analysis. I produced the analysis and the maps presented here.

Data and sources

NO2 and SO2 monitoring-station measurements, and the Auckland boundary.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I used Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) to create continuous concentration surfaces, clipped them to the Auckland boundary and symbolised each with a seven-class legend. I then reclassified the rasters to flag cells where NO2 exceeded 35 µg/m³ and SO2 exceeded 50 µg/m³, and multiplied the two binary layers to isolate cells where both thresholds were exceeded.

Output and interpretation

The outputs are a concentration surface for each pollutant and a map of cells above both selected thresholds. The surfaces are interpolated estimates between stations, not direct measurements, so the combined map shows where the model estimates both thresholds are exceeded.

Limitations

The 35 µg/m³ and 50 µg/m³ values are analysis thresholds I selected. Their regulatory basis and averaging periods have not been verified, so the output should not be read as a validated health-risk map.

Suitability and planningCompleted

Residential suitability in Botkyrka, Sweden

Which locations meet a defined set of residential suitability criteria within the Botkyrka study area?

  • QGIS
  • Distance rasters
  • Boolean constraints
  • Weighted overlay
Location
Botkyrka, Stockholm, Sweden
Software
QGIS
Outputs
A suitability index map with parcels, roads and transport context
Read the case study

Overview

A multi-criteria suitability model built in QGIS. It combines two weighted distance factors with Boolean constraints to show where the selected residential criteria are met. I produced the analysis and the maps presented here.

Data and sources

Spatial layers for water, roads, public transport access, parcel ownership and land use.

Method

I generated distance rasters for water and roads and normalised them to a 0–1 scale as two equally weighted factors. I then applied Boolean constraint layers for public transport access, parcel ownership and land use, together with water buffers, and combined everything in a weighted overlay using the Raster Calculator.

Output and interpretation

A final map of locations that meet the selected criteria. The result depends on the criteria and weights I chose: it shows where those criteria are met, not whether development would actually be feasible.

Limitations

Different criteria or weights would give a different result, so the model is best read as a transparent screening tool rather than a recommendation.

Suitability and planningCompleted

Land-use suitability and conflict in the Kathmandu Valley

Where do the modelled suitability surfaces for the carpet industry and for agriculture overlap, and so compete for the same land?

  • QGIS
  • Slope and aspect
  • Weighted criteria
  • Raster overlay
Location
Kathmandu Valley, Nepal
Software
QGIS
Outputs
Two suitability surfaces and a land-use conflict map
Read the case study

Overview

Two suitability models, one for the carpet industry and one for agriculture, overlaid to show where they could compete for the same land. I produced the analysis and the maps presented here.

Data and sources

A relief (elevation) layer, plus criteria layers for power and water.

Method

In QGIS, I derived slope and aspect from relief and normalised the criteria. I applied weighted overlays to produce two surfaces: carpet industry (weights 40/30/20/10) and agriculture (weights 60/40, plus constraints). I then overlaid them to flag cells where both surfaces scored highly.

Output and interpretation

In this alpine study area, the carpet-industry model favoured river valleys, while the agriculture model showed broader suitability on flatter, more accessible terrain. The conflict map shows where both score highly. These are modelled land-use conflicts, not evidence of actual disputes or development proposals.

Limitations

The overlap depends on the criteria and weights chosen for each model.

Environment and climateCompleted

Field measurements of temperature and humidity, turned into interpolated surfaces to compare the spatial patterns different methods and survey years produce.

  • Field measurements
  • IDW
  • Kriging
Location
Auckland Domain, Auckland
Methods
IDW and Kriging interpolation

Overview

Two pieces of microclimate work in the Auckland Domain: comparing interpolation methods for temperature, and comparing humidity surveys from 2025 and 2026.

Data and sources

Temperature and humidity field measurements from the Auckland Domain, with humidity measured in 2025 and 2026.

Method: temperature

I created temperature surfaces with both IDW and Kriging and compared the spatial patterns each method produced.

Method: humidity

I produced IDW humidity surfaces for the 2025 and 2026 measurements so the two surveys could be compared.

Output and interpretation

In my temperature maps, IDW produced more localised hotspots around measurement points, while Kriging produced a smoother surface. The humidity maps show spatial variation across the Domain for each survey and let the two periods be compared side by side.

Limitations

Without validation evidence, I don't claim either interpolation method is more accurate. Two survey periods are snapshots, so differences between them don't show long-term climate change.

Public transport and walking access in AucklandTransport and accessibility

Transport and accessibilityCompleted

Bus access around Mt Eden station and walking catchments around Grafton, Kingsland and Mt Eden stations, compared with population density.

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • Network analysis
  • Catchments
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro

Overview

Two pieces of access analysis combined: bus access associated with Mt Eden station, and walking catchments around three inner Auckland stations.

Data and sources

Bus routes and stops serving Mt Eden station, the walking network, and census population density.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I selected the bus routes serving Mt Eden station and generated a service catchment with network analysis. For walking, I ran a multi-facility network analysis to create pedestrian catchments around Grafton, Kingsland and Mt Eden stations. In both cases I overlaid population density to look at residential access.

Output and interpretation

A bus commuter catchment for Mt Eden station, and a map comparing the three walking catchments against population density.

Supermarket proximity and high-income areasUrban spatial analysis

Urban spatial analysisCompleted

Using 1 km buffers around Auckland supermarkets to investigate their proximity to high-income areas.

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • Attribute filtering
  • Buffers
  • Intersection
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro

Overview

A proximity analysis comparing supermarket locations with high-income areas in Auckland.

Data and sources

Supermarket locations from open data and income-by-area census layers, clipped to Auckland.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I filtered the census data to extract high-income areas, applied 1 km buffers around supermarket locations, and used intersection and spatial selection to find supermarkets near high-income areas.

Output and interpretation

A map of supermarket buffers in relation to high-income areas. Proximity alone does not show affordability, shopping behaviour or overall food accessibility.

Limitations

A 1 km buffer measures straight-line distance rather than travel along roads.

Food-business corridors in AucklandUrban spatial analysis

Urban spatial analysisCompleted

Mapping food businesses and aggregating them along the road network to find high-density corridors.

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • Spatial join
  • Dissolve
  • Attribute filtering
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro

Overview

An analysis of food-business concentration along Auckland roads.

Data and sources

Food-business locations and a road network.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I mapped food businesses by type and spatially joined the points to the road network. I dissolved road segments to count businesses per corridor, kept only corridors with 20 or more food businesses, and compared the full dataset with these high-density corridors.

Output and interpretation

Maps of all food businesses by type and of the high-density corridors. These show food-business concentration only. They say nothing about traffic, because no traffic data were analysed.

Limitations

The 20-business threshold is a cut-off I selected.

Outside my studies: terrariums

Building terrariums is my hobby. Each one is a small landscape in glass, planned with layers, hardscape, moss and plants, much like reading a real landscape at a smaller scale. Open any build below to see it and read how I designed it.

How I build a terrariumLayers and process

Every build stacks the same layers from the bottom up: a 1–3 cm drainage layer, a substrate barrier, a fine charcoal chip layer, the substrate mix, then hardscape and plants on top.

My four-step process

  1. Landscape planning. Try different layouts, positions, orientations and sizes of rocks. Larger hardscape tends to go in the background as the framework.
  2. Concept design. Select a few large plants as the main piece so it is more eye-catching. Disperse the greenery and cover empty spots to hide the soil.
  3. Colour contrast. Use at least three different colours to create a more immersive and realistic environment.
  4. Final product. Make sure every spot is covered and the plants are rooted, so the ecosystem can thrive and the plants get enough sunlight without being obstructed.
