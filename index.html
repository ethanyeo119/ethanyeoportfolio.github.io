About me

I'm Ethan, an Earth Science and GIS student at the University of Auckland, studying from 2025 to 2027.

I'm interested in how landscapes, ecosystems and communities interact, and in using spatial data to look at those questions clearly. My projects so far range from interpolating air pollution and microclimate data to suitability modelling, access analysis and reading physical landscapes.

Most of my coursework has been in ArcGIS Pro and QGIS, working with vector and raster data. I enjoy the practical side too: cleaning and preparing data, building workflows that can be repeated, and finishing with maps that are easy to read.

Alongside my degree, I'm a Healthy Waters and Flood Resilience intern at Auckland Council and previously supported wetland restoration and waste reduction as an intern with Waiheke Resources Trust. On campus, I sit on the general committee of Te Rōpū Taiao, the Environment Association, and co-lead the Linguistics and Language Society.

I hope to apply geospatial analysis to real-world environmental and land-use decisions, helping to solve environmental challenges and support smarter city planning.

Following the eagles: movement and seasonal hotspots

How do recorded bald eagle locations change with the seasons, and which environmental conditions and land uses are they associated with?

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • ArcGIS StoryMaps
  • Google Earth Engine
  • Heatmaps
Species
Bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus)
Region
Pacific Northwest to the Arctic coast of Canada
Period
Tracking records, 2018–2026
Software
ArcGIS Pro, Google Earth Engine, ArcGIS StoryMaps
Read the case study

Overview

An interactive ArcGIS StoryMaps narrative that follows tracked bald eagles from the Pacific Northwest to the Arctic coast. It maps where eagles were recorded between 2018 and 2026, follows one tagged eagle (identified in the tracking data as TERF07) from the Vancouver area towards Mackenzie Bay, and compares eagle activity with season, temperature, vegetation and land use. I produced the analysis, cartography and story, completed in October 2026.

Data and sources

Eagle location records from Movebank (Bald Eagle in the Pacific Northwest); NDVI and temperature layers processed in Google Earth Engine; and publicly available land-use shapefiles for agricultural, industrial and open-space areas. Basemaps are from ArcGIS Online.

Method

I mapped the records through time, then followed TERF07 from its last Vancouver-area record on 24 February 2021 to a point near Mackenzie Bay on 26 May 2021, summing the distances between consecutive GPS positions to estimate about 4,920 km over roughly 91 days. I then focused on 2023 and two study areas, Vancouver and Mackenzie Bay, counted records per season, and compared median temperature, NDVI and wind speed at the eagle locations. In ArcGIS Pro, I built density heatmaps over land-use polygons for the Delta neighbourhoods and summarised land-use proportions in a donut chart.

Output and interpretation

In the first study area in 2023, records peaked in spring (313), with 270 in winter, 265 in summer and 47 in autumn. Median temperatures at winter and spring locations were well below freezing (−33.0 °C and −17.4 °C) with low NDVI, so recorded locations were generally in cold areas with little green vegetation, while summer locations were warm (22.4 °C) and greener. In winter, points were more common over the warmer pockets of the study area, which may reflect the use of microclimates. In Delta, the densest hotspot falls within an industrial zone, even though industrial land makes up only 23.0% of the land use, compared with 46.0% agriculture and 31.0% open space.

Limitations

The points show where eagles were recorded, not what they were doing or whether they preferred those conditions over other available areas. The seasonal bars count records rather than individual eagles. Testing habitat preference properly would mean comparing eagle locations with all of the land available to them.

Residential suitability in Botkyrka, Sweden

Which locations meet a defined set of residential suitability criteria within the Botkyrka study area?

  • QGIS
  • Distance rasters
  • Boolean constraints
  • Weighted overlay
Location
Botkyrka, Stockholm, Sweden
Software
QGIS
Outputs
A suitability index map with parcels, roads and transport context
Read the case study

Overview

A multi-criteria suitability model built in QGIS. It combines two weighted distance factors with Boolean constraints to show where the selected residential criteria are met. I produced the analysis and the maps presented here.

Data and sources

Spatial layers for water, roads, public transport access, parcel ownership and land use.

Method

I generated distance rasters for water and roads and normalised them to a 0–1 scale as two equally weighted factors. I then applied Boolean constraint layers for public transport access, parcel ownership and land use, together with water buffers, and combined everything in a weighted overlay using the Raster Calculator.

Output and interpretation

A final map of locations that meet the selected criteria. The result depends on the criteria and weights I chose: it shows where those criteria are met, not whether development would actually be feasible.

Limitations

Different criteria or weights would give a different result, so the model is best read as a transparent screening tool rather than a recommendation.

Food businesses and high-traffic corridors in Auckland

Along which Auckland road corridors are food businesses most concentrated, and which corridors hold 20 or more?

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • Spatial join
  • Dissolve
  • Attribute filtering
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro
Outputs
Food businesses by type and a map of high-density corridors
Read the case study

Overview

An analysis of food-business concentration along Auckland's roads, identifying the corridors that function as major food strips. I produced the analysis and the maps presented here.

Data and sources

Food-business point locations, categorised by type (bar, cafe, fast food, food court, hotel, pub and restaurant), and a road network.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I mapped food businesses by type and spatially joined the points to the road network. I dissolved road segments to count businesses per corridor, kept only corridors with 20 or more food businesses, and compared the full dataset with these high-density corridors.

Output and interpretation

Maps of all food businesses by type and of the high-density corridors, which highlight the key food districts across the city. These show food-business concentration only. They say nothing about traffic volumes, because no traffic data were analysed.

Limitations

The 20-business threshold is a cut-off I selected, so a different threshold would identify a different set of corridors.

How can monitoring-station measurements be used to visualise spatial variation in NO2 and SO2 across Auckland?

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • IDW interpolation
  • Reclassification
  • Raster overlay
Location
Auckland, New Zealand
Software
ArcGIS Pro

Overview

I used readings from air quality monitoring stations to estimate how NO2 and SO2 vary across Auckland, then looked for places where both pollutants were above thresholds I selected for the analysis.

Question

How can monitoring-station measurements be used to visualise spatial variation in NO2 and SO2 across Auckland?

Data and sources

NO2 and SO2 monitoring-station measurements and the Auckland boundary.

Method

In ArcGIS Pro, I used Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) to create continuous concentration surfaces, clipped them to the Auckland boundary and symbolised each with a seven-class legend. I then reclassified the rasters to flag cells where NO2 was above 35 µg/m³ and SO2 was above 50 µg/m³, and multiplied the two binary layers to find cells where both were exceeded.

Output and interpretation

The outputs are a concentration surface for each pollutant and a map of cells above both selected thresholds. The surfaces are interpolated estimates between stations, not direct measurements, so the combined map shows where the model estimates both thresholds are exceeded.

Limitations

The 35 µg/m³ and 50 µg/m³ values are selected analysis thresholds. Their regulatory basis and averaging periods have not been verified here, so the output is not a validated health-risk map.

Environment and climateCompleted

Field measurements of temperature and humidity, turned into interpolated surfaces to compare the spatial patterns different methods and survey years produce.

  • Field measurements
  • IDW
  • Kriging
Location
Auckland Domain, Auckland
Methods
IDW and Kriging interpolation

Overview

Two pieces of microclimate work in the Auckland Domain: comparing interpolation methods for temperature, and comparing humidity surveys from 2025 and 2026.

Data and sources

Temperature and humidity field measurements from the Auckland Domain, with humidity measured in 2025 and 2026.

Method: temperature

I created temperature surfaces with both IDW and Kriging and compared the spatial patterns each method produced.

Method: humidity

I produced IDW humidity surfaces for the 2025 and 2026 measurements so the two surveys could be compared.

Output and interpretation

In my temperature maps, IDW produced more localised hotspots around measurement points, while Kriging produced a smoother surface. The humidity maps show spatial variation across the Domain for each survey and let the two periods be compared side by side.

Limitations

Without validation evidence, I don't claim either interpolation method is more accurate. Two survey periods are snapshots, so differences between them don't show long-term climate change.

Suitability and planningCompleted

Where do modelled suitability for carpet industry and agriculture overlap?

  • QGIS
  • Slope and aspect
  • Weighted criteria
  • Overlay
Location
Kathmandu Valley, Nepal
Software
QGIS

Overview

Two suitability models, one for carpet industry and one for agriculture, overlaid to show where they could compete for the same land.

Question

Where do modelled suitability for carpet industry and agriculture overlap?

Data and sources

A relief (elevation) layer plus criteria layers for power and water.

Method

In QGIS, I derived slope and aspect from relief and normalised the criteria. I applied weighted overlays to make two surfaces: carpet industry (weights 40/30/20/10) and agriculture (weights 60/40, plus constraints). I then overlaid them to flag cells where both surfaces scored highly.

Output and interpretation

In this alpine study area, the carpet-industry model favoured river valleys, while the agriculture model showed broader suitability on flatter, more accessible terrain. The conflict map shows where both score highly. These are modelled land-use conflicts, not evidence of actual disputes or existing development proposals.

Limitations

The overlap depends on the criteria and weights chosen for each model.

Landscape and Earth ScienceCompleted

What do the rocks, sediments and processes at four sites around the Waikato River mouth reveal about how the Port Waikato landscape formed and continues to change?

  • Fieldwork
  • Stratigraphy
  • Sediment analysis
  • Dumpy level survey
  • ArcGIS StoryMaps
Location
Port Waikato, New Zealand, about 80 km south of Auckland
Approach
Fieldwork presented as an ArcGIS StoryMaps story
Period
Field diagrams produced 2024–2025

Overview

A geological and ecological story of Port Waikato, where the Waikato River, New Zealand's longest, empties into the Tasman Sea. It brings together field observations from four sites: the river mouth, Sunset Beach, Waikawau Valley and Whakatiwai Stream. I produced the field diagrams, graphs and story.

Data and sources

Field sketches, stratigraphic columns, 3D cross-sections, stream cross-sections and surface-area graphs that I produced during University of Auckland fieldwork. Geological background comes from Te Ara, the Encyclopedia of New Zealand, and the New Zealand Geological Survey, and ecological data from Waikato Regional Council and Science Learning Hub.

Method

Sunset Beach: I collected sediment samples across the beach profile and analysed them by laser diffraction for grain size distribution, extracted hand-auger cores to log subsurface stratigraphy, and ran a dumpy level survey from the dune crest to the low tide mark. Waikawau Valley: I recorded and sketched the sedimentary sequence and documented the Ohuka Landslide. Whakatiwai Stream: I measured wetted width, depth and surface velocity to calculate cross-sectional area and discharge for each channel thread, and logged bed material, bank layers and bar features.

Output and interpretation

The beach survey provides a baseline elevation record for tracking erosion and accretion over time. At Waikawau Valley the rock layers dip gently eastward at about 8 degrees, from Jurassic greywacke basement through sandstone, siltstone and limestone to Quaternary alluvium, reflecting marine deposition followed by uplift. The Ohuka Landslide is a three-layer failure: a weaker, clay-rich siltstone sits between stronger rock, rainwater raises pore pressure in it, the upper layers slide, and coastal erosion removes support at the base. At Whakatiwai Stream, the discharge calculations show how different channel threads contribute to total flow, linking hydraulic conditions to sediment transport and channel stability.

Limitations

The measurements are a snapshot from fieldwork, so repeated surveys would be needed to show actual erosion and accretion over time.

Kaikōura earthquake: faults, aftershocks and landslides

How are the active faults, the aftershock sequence and the landslides around Kaikōura related, and what do they mean for the town and the transport routes along the coast?

  • ArcGIS Pro
  • Active faults
  • Aftershock mapping
  • Landslide mapping
Location
Kaikōura coast, New Zealand, including Hapuku, Oaro and Clarence
Event
M7.8 mainshock and its aftershocks (magnitudes 0.18 to 6.58)
Status
In progress

Objective

To see where the earthquake's effects were concentrated by comparing active fault traces, aftershock locations and sizes, and landslide source areas and debris trails, and to see how they line up with Kaikōura's roads, State Highway 1 and the railway.

Done so far

A study-area base map with terrain, settlements, roads, the railway and active faults. The mainshock and aftershocks mapped by magnitude. Landslide source areas and debris trails mapped across the surrounding hills.

Next steps

Quantify the patterns, for example landslide density against distance from active faults and aftershock clusters, and measure how much of State Highway 1 and the railway lies in or near mapped landslide areas.

Interests

Outside my studies

Outside my studies and work, my main interest is building terrariums. Each one is a small landscape in glass, planned with layers, hardscape, moss and plants, much like reading a real landscape at a smaller scale. Open any build below to see it and read how I designed it.

How I build a terrariumLayers and process

Every build stacks the same layers from the bottom up. A 1–3 cm drainage layer lets excess water collect away from the roots, a substrate barrier stops soil from washing down into it, and a fine charcoal chip layer helps keep the enclosed air fresh. The substrate mix sits on top, and the hardscape and plants go in last.

My four-step process

  1. Landscape planning. I try different layouts, positions, orientations and sizes of rock before committing. Larger hardscape usually goes in the background as the framework for the scene.
  2. Concept design. I choose a few large plants as the main piece, so the scene has a clear focal point. The remaining greenery is dispersed to cover empty spots and hide the soil.
  3. Colour contrast. I aim for at least three different colours, which makes the environment feel more immersive and realistic.
  4. Final product. Every spot is covered and every plant is rooted, so the ecosystem can thrive and each plant gets enough sunlight without being blocked by its neighbours.
